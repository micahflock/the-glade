## 1. The reframe, and what it actually fixes

The memo's pitch: the summer ring is not a heat waste, it's a **magical chaos waste**.
Still dry, hot, burnt — but the defining feature is that this close to the epicenter
the amplification has stopped being something that happens *to creatures* and started
being something that happens *to physics*. Nothing holds still. Nobody is in control.

This is a better idea than the heat version for four specific, non-vibes reasons:

**1. It closes an open question that is currently blocking.**
`Open_Questions.md` line 28: *"The ants established the shape — infiltration by
disguise, find the faction's internal fracture, and split it rather than fight it.
Does that same shape work for the summer termites and the Fall, or does it get
repetitive? Needs a second pattern before the next zone."*

A zone with no faction is *definitionally* not that pattern. There is nobody to
infiltrate, nobody to split, no queen to reach. The problem stops being social and
becomes spatial and attritional: get in, survive, get the thing, get out. That is the
second pattern, and it arrives exactly when the doc says it's needed.

**2. It fixes the patrol logic — as the memo says outright.**
"It's hot" does not stop an army; armies cross deserts. "It's unpredictable" does —
you cannot run a supply line through terrain that rewrites itself. It also makes the
Red Ant Queen look *shrewd* for turning back rather than lazy, which is better for a
faction the party is about to rob.

**3. The magic gradient finally cashes out.**
`Homebrew_Rules.md` already commits to "all magic amplified toward the epicenter" and
"what is X bug but MORE?" as the monster frame. Through winter and spring that has been
flavor plus stat bumps. Making it environmental is the first time the players will
*feel* the gradient rather than be told about it — and it earns the +3 jump to level 9.

**4. It sets up the Fall by opposition.**
Scorch: no one is in control. Fall: one thing controls everything, including the dead.
Going from total chaos to total, singular, cordyceps-mediated order is a much sharper
turn than desert → swamp. The Scorch makes the Black Queen scarier for free.

### The one thing the reframe costs

The zone loses its *antagonist*. Winter had earwig bandits and raiders; spring has the
alliance. A chaos waste has weather. That is a real risk of the concept: environmental
hazard is not a villain, and three sessions of "the ground is weird" gets old.

The fix is §6 (the survivors give it a human face) and §10 (the scorpion supply line
gives it an off-screen hand). Flagging it here so it doesn't get discovered at the table.

## 2. The name

"The Scorch" is now describing the least interesting half of the zone. It appears in
`s06_draft`, `s07_draft`, `s08_draft`, and `notes/session06ideas.md`.

**Recommended (zero cost): keep "the Scorch" as the *outsider* name.** It is what the
Overgrowth believes is out there — heat, fire, burnt ground — because scouts who got
one day in and turned back reported the first thing they saw. The party arrives and
finds the name is *wrong*, or at least badly incomplete. The wrongness of the name is
the reveal. No files change, and an inconsistency becomes a beat.

**Then give it a second, local name** — what the survivors who actually live there call
it. Party learns it when they meet them. Candidates:

| Name | Read |
| :--- | :--- |
| **The Wildfire** | Best pun in the set — reads as fire to anyone who hasn't been, means wild magic to anyone who has. The name itself performs the reveal. |
| **The Churn** | Grim, working-class, sounds like something survivors would actually say. Nothing holds still. |
| **The Riot** | Chaos without heat. Slightly too modern? |
| **The Squander** | Melancholy — a place where magic is *wasted*. Fits the campaign's weariness. |
| **The Shimmer** | Evocative but *Annihilation* owns it. |

Lean: **Scorch (public) / the Wildfire (local)**, with the Churn as the runner-up if
the pun reads too cute at the table.

## 3. What the zone actually is

**The physics is unreliable, and that's the whole zone.** Not "spells go wrong" —
*ambient reality is drifting*. Concretely, the three signals in order of when the party
meets them:

1. **Water refuses the ground.** (Already written into `s08_draft` §B — keep it exactly
   as pitched.) The first sign is not a monster, it's a waterskin missing its mark and
   the spill beading up and rolling away like mercury. Beautiful cold open.
2. **Small things are powerful.** The bottom of the food chain got the magic. An aphid
   — livestock, background, *food* for the entire campaign so far — is now a hazard.
   This is the memo's core image and it's the strongest one in the pitch (see §9).
3. **The land does not stay put.** Not earthquakes: *revision*. You come back through a
   pass you crossed yesterday and it is a different pass. This is the mechanical
   keystone for §7 — it means the DMs never author the same room twice and the players
   never feel like they're backtracking.

**No enclaves, no cities.** Per the memo. The only settled thing is the survivor
holdfast (§6), and it is not a town — it is people refusing to leave, in a structure
built for a hundred times their number.

**Where the horror sits.** Campaign tone statement is *"horror announces itself in
absences."* The Scorch's absence is **succession** — nothing is anything's child. The
warped things here didn't inherit their shapes, they were *assigned* them, at random,
recently. There are no families in the Scorch. That's the thing that should make the
party uneasy before anything attacks them.

### The termite reconciliation

"No one is in control" kills the termite *faction*. It does not have to kill the
termites, and it definitely shouldn't kill the mound — the tropes list wants that
dungeon and it's the natural home for the 1e gauntlet.

**Resolution: the termites were the faction, and they lost.**

The mound is still the largest structure in the ring. It is abandoned, or worse. This
does three things at once:

- **It's your gauntlet container** — a megastructure built for millions, now holding a
  few hundred wrong things. That's a dungeon with a reason to be big, a reason to be
  full of loot, and a reason nobody cleaned it out.
- **It evidences the emptiness instead of asserting it.** The party doesn't get told
  nobody rules the Scorch; they walk through the ruins of the last thing that tried.
- **It bridges to the Fall.** Real fungus-growing termites (*Macrotermitinae*) cultivate
  *Termitomyces* in fungus combs — they are the ecological twin of the leafcutters. So
  the mound is **the Scorch's Winter Farm**: a fungus operation that failed, badly.
  The chain is now clean:

  > Leafcutters farm fungus (spring) → termites farmed fungus and it ate them (summer)
  > → cordyceps farms everything (fall)

  And the party sees, in the mound, a fungus that grows on the *living* — the exact
  thing the Leafcutter Queen has been failing to make. The Scorch already did it. That
  is a hell of a payoff for a thread planted in Session 6.

Real *Macrotermes* mounds are passive ventilation engines that self-regulate internal
temperature. So the mound is the only cool place in the zone, and it's cool because it
still **breathes**. That's a dungeon feature, a hazard system, and a horror image in
one: the building is respirating and its occupants are dead.

## 5. What is the party doing there?

Not in the memo, and it's the thing that makes or breaks a gauntlet. In the Overgrowth
they had a mandate (reach the Leafcutter Queen). If the Scorch is just transit, depth
has no pull and the crawl is a chore.

| Objective | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- |
| Cross it | Weakest. Pure transit. Depth is punished, not rewarded. |
| Find something in it (the Black Queen's trail, scout intel) | Fine, thin. |
| Get something *out* of it for someone else | Good — gives the survivors leverage. |
| **The way into the Fall is closed, and the only thing that opens it is deep in the Scorch** | **Recommended.** Makes the gauntlet mandatory and makes depth *mean* something. The deeper you go, the closer to the thing. Classic, and it works. |

The Leafcutter Queen is the natural source of this in Session 8 — she sent scouts, they
came back with a problem, not a route.

## 6. The grim survivors

The memo's "grim survivors that are somehow able to protect themselves." They carry more
weight than their screen time suggests: in a zone with no faction, **the only social
scene in the ring is enormous by default.** They're also the fix for the "no antagonist"
risk in §1.

- **Not a species — a remnant.** Mixed. Whoever was left. That's a texture the campaign
  hasn't used yet; every settlement so far has been species-coded (Canopia = pollinators,
  Weevilville = detritivores).
- **Suggested core:** harvester ants (*Pogonomyrmex* — obsessive seed-cachers, so they
  survived because they had *stores*; also the most painful sting in North America, so
  they can defend a door) plus fog-basking darkling beetles (*Stenocara* — real beetles
  that harvest water from fog on their own backs, which in a zone where water rolls away
  makes them walking wells).
- **What they do:** they run the honeydew harvest (§9). That's their livelihood, it's
  the only renewable resource in a dead ring, and it's insanely dangerous. Gives them a
  reason to exist that isn't "they're tough."
- **Their attitude toward the party:** the good version is not hostility, it's
  *actuarial*. They've watched people walk in before. They are not impressed and they
  are not cruel — they'll trade, they'll warn you once, and they will not come get you.
  Grounded, not dreary; per the tone statement.
- **They are the hub** if you take Option A in §7.

## 7. The roguelike question

The honest constraints, up front:

- ~10 sessions planned; they're on 8. The Scorch realistically gets **2 sessions, maybe 3.**
- Codex says the ring is ~2 days wide.
- Party of three, level 9.
- A real roguelike needs *many* runs to feel like one. There aren't many.
- Losing a level-9 PC to a randomizer is not going to be fun for anyone at this table.

**So: don't build a roguelike. Build one run of a roguelike, structured so it feels
like the third run.** The parts of the genre that actually produce the good feeling are
separable from the death-and-reset loop, and they're the cheap parts.

### Option A — The Push *(recommended)*

**You don't reset by dying. You reset by turning back.**

- **Hub:** the survivor holdfast at the edge (or a fortified wing of the termite mound).
- **A run** = one push into the Scorch, ~4–6 beats, roughly a session.
- **The gauge:** you can only long rest inside the tent, and the tent is limited (charges,
  or it needs something to recharge that you find inside). Hard, visible fuel meter.
- **The choice, every beat:** push deeper for better loot and more progress, or turn back
  now and keep what you're carrying.
- **Why going back in isn't backtracking:** the zone rearranges. Diegetic — the magic
  won't hold still. This is the trick that makes the whole thing work. The DMs never
  author the same room twice, and the players never re-walk a cleared corridor.
- **Meta-progression:** the holdfast improves with what you bring back. Bring water, the
  cistern opens. Bring a stabilized reagent, they can ward your gear. This is the Hades
  house / Dead Cells hub, and it gives the DMs a permanent place to hang NPCs and shops.
- **No PC death required.** The "death" is the failed run — you lose resources, time, and
  a thread you wanted, not a character.
- **Fits the budget:** 2 sessions = 2–3 runs, which is exactly enough to *feel* the loop
  without it becoming the campaign.

### Option B — The Gauntlet (pure 1e)

One-way. Enter here, exit there, the way back closes behind you (the wavefront shifted;
the ground revised). No long rests, or exactly one. Every resource comes from what you
find.

Most 1e-authentic and the tensest. Also the most likely to genuinely TPK, and the *least*
roguelike — no runs, no meta-progression, no build. **Best if what's wanted is one great
session rather than a sub-arc.**

### Option C — Actual resets, with actual time magic

Worth taking seriously rather than dismissing, because the campaign's own lore already
supplies the mechanism: **time magic is the forbidden magic** (`s08_draft` §A). The Black
Queen froze time; the ring nearest the epicenter is soaked in the overspill. A loop is
diegetically *available* here in a way it is in almost no other D&D campaign.

The case against: it undercuts death stakes going into the endgame, and it spends the
time-magic reveal in the Scorch instead of at the Black Queen.

**Middle path worth considering:** the loop happens **once**, to **one character**, as a
scripted beat rather than a mechanic. A PC drops to 0 and *rewinds* — comes back six
seconds earlier, carrying the memory. It happens one time, it is never explained, and the
party spends the rest of the campaign wondering what they saw. That buys the roguelike
frisson at zero mechanical cost and is a hell of a foreshadow for the Black Queen.

### The wild magic table — one strong recommendation

Whatever structure wins: **do not trigger surges off the party casting spells.** If
casting rolls on a chaos table, Nancy and Drix stop casting, and the zone punishes the
players for playing their characters.

Trigger surges off **the world** instead — a round timer, damage thresholds, terrain,
enemy abilities. Then chaos is a shared hazard everyone has to fight *around*, not a tax
on the casters. Write ~20 bespoke entries that land on the *battlefield* rather than on a
person (the PHB sorcerer table is mostly personal and mostly duds).

## 8. Encounter design shapes

Six distinct shapes rather than a monster list. Each answers a specific need, and under
Option A each is a card type in the run deck.

Note the standing constraint from `s08_draft` §C: **Nancy is very hard to pin down**
(bonus-action disengage, 40 speed, high AC) and *"will fuck up the game in an arena."*
The DMs asked for interesting terrain and secondary objectives. Every shape below is
built to answer that — and note that shape 2 makes her mobility an *asset* rather than a
problem, which is a better fix than nerfing her.

**1. The Attrition Wave.** *(the aphids)*
Not a boss — a tide. Individually trivial, functionally endless. The objective is never
"kill them all," it's "finish the task before you're covered." Nancy's mobility doesn't
help, because the threat is *area*, not a target. Very 1e: the wandering encounter you're
supposed to run from.

**2. The Terrain Is The Monster.**
The antagonist is a place. A surge field, a collapsing thermal, a floor that revises
itself every round. Enemies are incidental. This is the direct implementation of the
"interesting terrain" ask, and it's the one where being the fast one with a bonus-action
disengage is the *answer* rather than the problem.

**3. The Stabilizer.**
One enemy whose entire deal is that it *suppresses* the chaos in a radius. Inside its
aura, magic works normally. Outside, everything surges. So: fight it on its terms in the
bubble (fair fight, its stats), or drag it out into the chaos (hurts everyone, including
you). A secondary-objective fight with a real dial the players control. Best shape in the
list.

**4. The Push-Your-Luck Room.**
Obvious loot, escalating cost per round you stay. Either no enemy at all, or one arriving
on a visible clock. Pure decision tension, near-zero combat math — excellent pacing filler
between real fights, and it's the most 1e thing on this list.

**5. The Apex.** *(the scorpion pair)*
Two bodies, one fight, and the only *coherent* things in the ring. Two adults hunting as
a unit — **no brood on screen**, per the §4 caution.

**6. The Social One.** *(the survivors)*
The danger is that they might be *right* about the party being a liability. One of these
per run, minimum.

## 9. Creature concepts

Sketches, not stat blocks — run the picks through `/magic-bug-generator` for the tier
gradients, then `/stat-block` for YAML. All grounded in real species, matching how
`creatures/magical-bugs.md` is built.

### ⭐ The aphids — the memo's own pitch, and the best thing in it

*Aphis nerii* (oleander aphid) — vivid yellow, black-legged, clusters in dense mats, and
**toxic**: it sequesters cardiac glycosides from its host plant.

Why this is the zone's signature creature: **the party has known aphids as livestock for
the entire campaign.** They're food. They're a supply chain. The Overgrowth *farms* them.
In the Scorch the livestock got the magic. The thing at the bottom of the food chain is
now the hazard. That's the reframe in one image, and it needs no explanation at the table.

Real biology that does all the mechanical work for free:

- **Parthenogenetic.** Clonal. No males needed for most of the life cycle — so the mass
  has **no mother**, which sidesteps the recurring-motif problem entirely rather than
  working around it.
- **Telescoping generations.** A newborn aphid is *already carrying her own developing
  daughters*. Kill one and you kill three generations at once. Which means the obvious
  fantasy inversion is right there: **every one that dies splits**, because the daughters
  were already inside. Killing them is how you lose. That's a genuinely novel combat
  problem and it's exactly the "don't just kill the thing" brief.
- **Each death fires a surge.** Small, random, environmental. The battlefield degrades as
  a function of how hard the party fights. Beautiful.
- **Alate morphs under crowding stress.** Real aphids grow *winged* forms when the colony
  gets too dense. That's your built-in alarm clock: when the party sees wings starting to
  appear in the mass, the swarm is about to disperse — and then it's not their problem
  anymore, it's the whole region's.
- **Honeydew.** Aphids excrete sugar; ants farm and milk them. Here the honeydew is
  **magically charged** — and that is the Scorch's entire consumable economy:

  > Drink the honeydew: random buff, random surge. Push-your-luck in a bottle.
  > It's what the survivors harvest. It's what the runs are *for*.
  > It's the reason the party doesn't just flee the aphid encounter.

  And it plays directly against **Life Pitch** (`Homebrew_Rules.md`): LP is money that is
  also HP — stable, expensive, safe. Honeydew is cheap, renewable, and dangerous. Two
  currencies with opposite risk profiles, under attrition, in a zone with no shops. That
  is a *very* good economy, and LP has never once come up at the table — this is the place
  for it.

Working names: **the Bloomherd**, **the Milk-Mad**, **the Surge-Fed**.

## 10. The connection already sitting in canon — use this

In **Session 7**, at the palace approach, the party saw carts of strange reagents:
juniper berries, glowing goop, and — **scorpion stingers.** It's in `campaign_state.yaml`
as a live thread and in `World_Codex.md` under the palace.

Scorpions are the Scorch apex. So:

> **Somebody is bringing scorpion parts out of the Scorch and selling them to the
> Red Ant Queen's lab.**

That is free, it is already canon, the party has *already seen it with their own eyes*,
and it is currently doing nothing. It gives the ring:

- **an entry NPC** — whoever runs that line can survive the Scorch, which makes them the
  guide, the smuggler, and the party's way in,
- **a reason the Scorch isn't hermetically sealed** despite the patrols turning back —
  an army can't cross it, but one very good, very lucky specialist can,
- **a thread that ties the Session 8 heist forward into the Scorch** rather than the two
  arcs sitting side by side,
- and a live question the party will *want* answered: what is the Queen building out of
  apex-predator parts?

Strongly recommend wiring this in. It costs nothing and it's the tightest link available
between the heist and the next ring.
